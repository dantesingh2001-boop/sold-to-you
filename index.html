THE SURVEY

We gave 126 students
four questions.

Anonymous slips, one sitting, students aged 16 to 21. The answers were not flattering to anyone, least of all to us. Every number on this page comes from that stack of paper.

Could name an ad they saw in the last hour

91%  ·  115 of 126

Remembered the product better than the story

68%  ·  86 of 126

Asked for something because someone online had it

54%  ·  68 of 126

Felt worse about how they look after scrolling

61%  ·  77 of 126

An ad never asks you to buy a product. It asks you to become a slightly different person, and then offers to sell you the receipt.

TEN BREAKDOWNS

Ten ads. Ten tricks.

Each one gets a page in the print issue: what it shows, what it promises, and what it quietly teaches a sixteen-year-old.

PAGE 04

Andar se young. Bahar se deadline.

Two women are mistaken for sisters. The joke sets a rule: a woman's age is a leak, not a chapter. The soap is aimed at women in their thirties. The people memorising it are fifteen.

PAGE 05

Kuch khaas hai. So khaas, you forgot it's a product.

Cadbury did not sell chocolate here. It sold permission, at a festival, an apology, a first salary. The wrapper is always in shot, and the price never is.

PAGE 06

The label changed. Did we?

In 2020, fairness became glow. Same bottle, gentler adjective. A rule against colour-shaming ads arrived in 2014 and the promise simply moved into understatement.

PAGE 07

One spray. Zero girlfriends.

Spray, and the room turns. Body odour is a hygiene problem and confidence is not in a can. Count the extras, then count your own circle. Subtract.

PAGE 08

Drink yourself beautiful. Terms apply.

A bright kitchen, a cup, sunlight, and words like detox, glow and light. Counted: “light” three times, “tea” twice. Your liver does the detox for free.

PAGE 09

Sweet without sugar. Guilt without end.

Two steps: fear, then rescue. One word — zero — lifts the whole packet two inches above other food. Nobody in the ad eats three servings.

PAGE 10

The blue liquid problem.

Television never got comfortable with red, so pads are demonstrated with blue ink. 77.3% of women aged 15–24 now use hygienic protection, up from 57.6% five years earlier.

PAGE 11

Long, black, and nothing to do with oil.

Hair is a scoreboard. The champi is the real memory the ad borrows, and it is a good memory. Oiling conditions hair; it does not grow more of it.

PAGE 12

The tune that outlived the product.

Before influencers there were jingles. A sewing machine meant a woman could earn from home. Forty years later, the tune still does the selling.

PAGE 13

A wedding, an argument, and a diamond.

One film about an inter-faith wedding was praised and pulled inside a week. Weddings are where advertising says out loud what family is supposed to be.

THE TOOLKIT

Seven buttons
every ad presses.

1
Borrowed love. The product sits inside a family moment so the feeling gets billed to the brand.
2
The mirror promise. A slightly better you, then a gap to close.
3
The invisible crowd. Everyone on screen already uses it. Real populations don't behave like ad populations.
4
Fear, then rescue. Create the worry, hand over the cure, in thirty seconds.
5
Small print maths. “Up to”, “helps”, “visibly”, “in 8 weeks”.
6
The celebrity hand-off. A face you trust lent to a product you cannot check.
7
The nostalgia loop. An old song. Your memory does the persuasion for free.

REALITY
